Plain-English explainer · The Sleeping Cloud project

A cloud for software with five users

AI can now write a little app for your team in minutes. This is the machine that makes sharing that app as easy as sharing a document — explained without the jargon.

About a 7-minute read. The technical versions of everything here exist; this page is the one you send to a human.

The problem

Software just got small. The cloud didn't notice.

For fifty years, software was only worth building if lots of people would use it. Nobody was going to spend six months coding a tracker for your team's lunch orders, or a sprint board that works exactly the way your team runs sprints. So we all squeezed into big one-size-fits-all products instead.

AI assistants quietly broke that rule. Today you can describe a little tool in plain English and have working software twenty minutes later — software with an audience of five. Building it is now the easy part.

Sharing it is still stuck in 2005. To get that lunch tracker in front of your teammates you need a server, a deployment pipeline, a login system, and a security review — the full costume of a real software company, for something the size of a spreadsheet. It's like being able to write a letter in seconds but needing to build a post office to mail it.

Meanwhile, sharing a document is one click. That's the standard this project holds itself to: a little app should be as easy to share — and as cheap to keep — as a Google Doc.

The core trick

Apps that sleep

Here's the thing about small software: it's almost always idle. Your team's tracker gets used maybe ten minutes a day. Renting it a full-time server is like leaving your car running in the driveway all week for two trips to the store.

So this cloud does what your laptop does when you close the lid. When nobody's using an app, we take a snapshot — a complete photograph of everything in its memory, mid-thought — save it to disk, and switch the app off. It costs nothing to run, because it isn't running. When someone opens the link, we restore the photograph and the app picks up exactly where it left off, in a fraction of a second. It never knows it was asleep.

The deeper an app sleeps, the cheaper it gets and the longer the yawn when it wakes:

Apps drift rightward as they cool, and jump all the way back the moment anyone clicks. Nothing is ever thrown away — at a third of a cent a month, there's no reason to delete anything.

A sleeping app costs about a third of a cent per month.That single number is what makes "share it like a doc" a real price instead of a slogan.

The experience

What happens when you click a shared link

Say a colleague built a vacation-day tracker and shared it with you. Weeks later, you click the link. The app has been in deep sleep the whole time. Here's the journey your click takes:

You click

The link opens like any web page.

The front desk checks you in

Your normal work login — no new password, no sign-up form.

Your request waits a moment

The app is asleep, so we hold your click at the door…

The app wakes up

…restore it from its snapshot, mid-thought, exactly as it was…

You're in

…and hand it your click. One slightly slow page load. Never an error.

While anyone's using it, the app stays awake and feels instant. A few minutes after the last person leaves, it quietly goes back to sleep. You never think about any of this — which is the point.

Sharing

It shares like a document, because it should

Here's the part that makes sharing AI-written software safe: the app itself contains no login code at all. Zero. The platform stands at the front door like a doorman — it checks who you are, checks the guest list, and only then lets your request through, wearing a name tag the app can trust.

That guest list looks exactly like the one you already know:

Share “Vacation Tracker”

People with access

A Anyone at acme.comyour whole company Can use
S Samsam@acme.com · built it Can edit
◦ The data insidewho submitted what Owner only
🔗 Anyone with the link can use — copy link

Not a mock-up of some future dream — this is the entire permission model.

Three things are worth noticing. “Can edit” includes AI: giving someone edit access increasingly means their AI assistant can change the app too, so it's a real decision, not a checkbox. The data has its own row: being allowed to use an app doesn't mean you can read everything other people typed into it. And un-sharing works instantly — because every visit passes the doorman, removing someone takes effect on their very next click. No passwords to rotate, nothing to redeploy.

One more quiet safety property: since your company login never touches the app's code — code an AI wrote and nobody read line-by-line — a badly behaved app can't steal what it never sees.

Data safety

The deli-ticket rule, or: why confusion can't corrupt your data

Every app keeps its records in its own little notebook (a small private database). The classic way clouds destroy data is embarrassingly mundane: a machine freezes or loses its network connection, the system moves the app to a healthy machine, and then the frozen one wakes up convinced it's still in charge — and both copies write to the notebook at once. The damage is silent and you find out weeks later.

On a cloud where apps are deliberately frozen and thawed thousands of times a day, that failure would be the normal case, not the freak accident. So the rule here is mechanical, like tickets at a deli counter:

Machine A
№ 41

Takes ticket 41, runs the app, writes happily in the notebook — then freezes mid-sentence.

Machine B
№ 42

The counter notices A went quiet, hands ticket 42 to machine B. B carries on from A's last completed line.

Machine A, three weeks later
№ 41

Wakes up and tries to write. The notebook checks the ticket: expired. Politely turned away. No harm done.

The tickets only ever count upward, and it's the notebook itself that checks them — not the machines, which can be confused, frozen, or (since AI wrote the app) simply not trusted. A machine can sleep for a month and wake up supremely confident; confidence doesn't beat an expired ticket.

And because every change is written into a journal before it counts, the notebook can be rebuilt as of any moment. Your AI assistant rewrote the app on Tuesday and mangled the data? Roll back to Tuesday, 2:59 pm — the same way you'd restore an old version of a document. Undo is not a feature bolted on top; it falls straight out of how the data is stored.

Confidence

We let a machine try to break it

Rules like the deli ticket sound airtight in a paragraph. The failures live in the weird orderings — what if the machine freezes after taking a ticket but before writing anything, and wakes up mid-handover? Humans are bad at imagining all of those. So we didn't rely on imagining.

First, we wrote out nine deliberate sabotage scenarios — frozen machines, split networks, stale snapshots — and walked each one through the rules on paper. Then we went further: we described the whole system in a mathematical language and had a model checker — a program whose only job is to try every possible ordering of events — explore roughly 27,000 distinct situations. Every safety rule held in all of them.

Two details make that more than a rubber stamp. When we deliberately removed a safety rule and re-ran the checker, it immediately produced the exact disaster that rule prevents — proof the rules are load-bearing, not decorative. And the checker found one failure story the humans had missed entirely: a machine that stalls at the worst possible microsecond during wake-up, in a way that only one specific rule catches. We'd written that rule as a belt-and-suspenders extra. It turned out to be the only thing standing there.

The humans found eight ways to break it. The machine found the ninth.Which is exactly why the machine stays in the loop for every future change.

The economics

Why the math works

Everything above compounds into one financial fact: apps that sleep are so cheap to keep that the platform can afford to host enormous numbers of them. Modeled at a million apps:

⅓¢
per month to keep a sleeping app — cheap enough to never delete anything
~$20k
monthly infrastructure for one million apps — about two dozen servers
17×
cheaper than the traditional approach of keeping every app running
¼ sec
wake-up target — the pause you barely notice on that first click

Honest label: these are modeled numbers, not measured ones — with one exception in progress. The wake-up speed is the assumption everything else leans on, so a hardware benchmark is scripted and ready to run for about $25 of cloud time.

snapshotThe photograph of a sleeping app's memory — everything, mid-thought — that lets it wake up exactly where it left off.
microVMThe tiny sealed computer-within-a-computer each app runs in, so a thousand strangers' apps can share one machine without touching each other.
epochThe deli-ticket number. Only the newest ticket may write; the numbers only ever count up.
fencingThe act of turning away an expired ticket — done by the notebook, never by trusting the machine.
